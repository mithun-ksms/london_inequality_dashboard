London is one of the wealthiest cities in the world, yet stark inequality remains an undeniable reality across its 32 boroughs. Life expectancy differs by up to 10 years between the most and least deprived areas, and average income in wealthy boroughs such as Kensington and Chelsea is reported to be five times higher than in Barking and Dagenham — despite residents across all boroughs contributing similarly through council tax and other forms of taxation. These disparities highlight a fundamental tension between economic prosperity and social equity within one of the world's most prominent global cities.

Despite the scale of this inequality, existing approaches to measuring deprivation often examine factors in isolation. While government indices provide valuable data across domains such as income, employment, and health, these measures are updated infrequently and fail to capture how multiple deprivation indicators interact with one another. A borough may score favourably on income metrics yet perform poorly on air quality or educational attainment — and it is the combination of these factors, rather than any single metric, that most accurately reflects the lived experience of deprivation.
This study proposes an AI-driven analytical framework that integrates four publicly available datasets covering deprivation indices, crime rates, air quality, and educational outcomes across London boroughs. By training a predictive machine learning model and visualising results through an interactive geospatial dashboard, this research aims to make complex socioeconomic analysis accessible not only to statisticians and policymakers, but to anyone seeking to understand and address inequality in London.
Specifically, this study seeks to address the following research questions:
* RQ1: Which socioeconomic factors most strongly predict deprivation scores across London boroughs?
* RQ2: Can a machine learning model accurately predict overall deprivation from a combination of socioeconomic indicators?
* RQ3: What spatial patterns emerge when multiple deprivation indicators are mapped at borough level?




2. Literature Review
2.1 Measuring Urban Deprivation
Measuring deprivation in England is not a new challenge. The English Indices of Deprivation 2019 is the sixth version of an official government measure that ranks every small neighbourhood in England based on how disadvantaged it is across seven areas of life — income, employment, education, health, crime, housing, and the local environment. It has followed the same core methodology since 2000, which makes it one of the most consistent and trusted tools for understanding poverty patterns over time. Income and employment carry the highest weightings at 22.5% each, which reflects how strongly financial insecurity shapes wider disadvantage. However, the index has a notable limitation — it shows where deprivation exists but does not reveal which factors are driving it most, or how different domains interact with one another. Addressing this limitation is one of the core motivations for this study.
2.2 Machine Learning and Socioeconomic Deprivation
Researchers have increasingly turned to machine learning to deepen our understanding of urban deprivation. Arribas-Bel et al. (2017) applied ML to predict living environment deprivation scores across Liverpool using satellite imagery, finding that machine learning outperformed traditional statistical methods and could handle far more variables simultaneously. Their work suggested that ML is particularly well-suited to the complexity of socioeconomic data, where many factors interact in ways that simpler models cannot capture. Building on this, Wan and Su (2022) compared multiple algorithms for inferring neighbourhood socioeconomic status and found that tree-based models — particularly Random Forest — consistently produced the strongest results. A recurring theme across this literature is that combining multiple deprivation indicators, rather than relying on a single measure, leads to more accurate and meaningful analysis. This study follows that principle by integrating deprivation domain scores with recorded crime data across all 32 London boroughs.
2.3 Explainable AI in Policy
One of the most common criticisms of machine learning in public policy is that it functions as a black box — it produces predictions without explaining how it arrived at them. In the context of deprivation analysis, this is a real problem. Policymakers need to know not just which boroughs are struggling, but why — whether it is driven by unemployment, poor health outcomes, inadequate housing, or some combination of factors. SHAP, introduced by Lundberg and Lee (2017), addresses this by attributing each prediction to its contributing features, making it possible to see exactly what the model is responding to. Bell et al. (2022) further showed that the perceived trade-off between model accuracy and explainability is less straightforward than often assumed — explainable models can perform just as well as black-box alternatives in policy settings. This study incorporates SHAP analysis to ensure the model's findings are interpretable not only for researchers but for anyone engaging with the dashboard.
2.4 Inequality Across London Boroughs
London's inequality is well-documented but remains a persistent challenge. Higgins et al. (2014) analysed quality of life across London boroughs using a wide range of government indicators, finding that deprivation, health, and educational inequalities were the defining differences between boroughs — not transport or community safety as is sometimes assumed. Importantly, their study highlighted that broad comparisons between inner and outer London tend to mask the real depth of inequality at borough level. Research by the Centre for London (2022) reinforces this, showing that low pay and unemployment cluster in specific boroughs rather than being distributed evenly across the city. Even within a single borough, disparities can be significant — studies of Kensington and Chelsea found that life satisfaction and overcrowding rates varied dramatically between its wealthier and more deprived wards. These findings confirm that borough-level analysis, while a useful starting point, still involves a degree of generalisation that future research at finer geographic scales could address.
2.5 Research Gap
Despite this growing body of work, three gaps remain. First, most machine learning studies on deprivation use satellite imagery or social media rather than official government multi-domain indices. Second, very few studies apply explainable AI to London specifically at borough level. Third, existing research on London inequality tends to describe patterns rather than predict them. This study addresses all three gaps by combining the IoD2019 with Metropolitan Police crime data, applying a Random Forest model with SHAP-based explainability, and presenting the results through an interactive geospatial dashboard designed to be accessible beyond the research community.

3. Methodology
3.1 Data Sources
This study draws on two publicly available government datasets. The primary source is the English Indices of Deprivation 2019, published by the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, which provides deprivation scores across seven domains for every Lower Super Output Area in England. The second dataset is the Metropolitan Police Service Borough Level Crime data, covering all recorded crimes across London's 32 boroughs from July 2024 to June 2026, broken down by crime category and month. Borough boundary files from the London Datastore were also obtained for geospatial visualisation purposes.
3.2 Data Preprocessing
The IoD2019 dataset originally contained 32,844 rows at LSOA level, covering all of England. These were filtered to retain only the 32 London boroughs, producing approximately 4,900 rows. These were then aggregated to borough level using mean values across all LSOAs within each borough, yielding a 32-row dataset with seven domain scores per borough. The crime dataset was processed by summing all crime categories across all 24 months per borough to produce a single total crime count figure. An 'Unknown' entry — representing crimes with unrecorded locations — was identified and removed prior to merging. The two datasets were joined on borough name using an inner join, producing a final master dataset of 32 rows and 9 features.
3.3 Exploratory Data Analysis
Before modelling, a correlation heatmap was generated across all features. This revealed that income deprivation and employment deprivation were almost perfectly correlated (r = 0.97), suggesting they move together across London boroughs. Overall deprivation score showed the strongest correlation with income (r = 0.98) and employment (r = 0.96). Notably, total recorded crime count showed a relatively weak correlation with overall deprivation (r = 0.40), likely reflecting high-footfall areas such as Westminster where crime volumes are elevated independent of local deprivation levels.
3.4 Model Selection and Training
A Random Forest Regressor was selected as the predictive model for three reasons. First, it performs well on small structured datasets — the 32-borough sample size is a constraint that tree-based ensemble methods handle better than linear models or neural networks. Second, it captures non-linear relationships between features, which is important given the complex interactions between deprivation domains. Third, it is directly compatible with SHAP-based explainability analysis, which is central to this study's research objectives. The model was trained using an 80/20 train-test split — 25 boroughs for training and 7 for testing — with 100 estimators and a fixed random state for reproducibility.
3.5 Explainability with SHAP
To interpret the model's predictions, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was applied using a TreeExplainer. SHAP values were calculated for all 32 boroughs across all features, producing both a feature importance bar chart and a detailed dot plot showing the direction and magnitude of each feature's contribution to individual predictions.

4. Results and Analysis
4.1 Model Performance
The Random Forest model achieved an R² score of 0.897 on the held-out test set, indicating that 89.7% of the variance in borough-level deprivation scores is explained by the selected socioeconomic indicators. The root mean squared error of 1.404 suggests that predictions deviate from actual deprivation scores by approximately 1.4 points on average. Given the small sample size of 32 boroughs, this represents strong predictive performance and confirms that the selected features are meaningful predictors of overall deprivation.
4.2 Feature Importance — What Drives Deprivation Most?
SHAP analysis identified employment score as the single most important predictor of overall deprivation across London boroughs, with a mean absolute SHAP value of approximately 1.9 — significantly higher than any other feature. Income score ranked second at approximately 1.55. Crime domain score ranked third at approximately 1.05, followed by health score at 0.45. Education score, total crime count, and environment score contributed comparatively little to the model's predictions.
This finding suggests that worklessness — the inability to access employment rather than simply having low wages — is the primary structural driver of deprivation across London boroughs. Income and employment together account for the overwhelming majority of the model's predictive power, which aligns with the weightings applied in the IoD2019 methodology itself.
4.3 Borough Deprivation Ranking
The borough ranking chart reveals a clear geographic pattern in London's inequality. Barking and Dagenham and Hackney emerged as the most deprived boroughs, both scoring above 32 on the overall deprivation index. Newham, Tower Hamlets, Islington, and Haringey formed the next cluster, all scoring above 27. At the other end, Richmond upon Thames scored the lowest at approximately 9.5, followed by Kingston upon Thames and Sutton. This east-west divide in London deprivation is well-documented in the existing literature and is confirmed by this study's findings.
4.4 Employment and Deprivation — A Direct Relationship
The employment vs deprivation scatter plot provides visual confirmation of the SHAP finding. Boroughs with higher employment deprivation scores cluster in the upper right of the chart — Barking and Dagenham, Hackney, and Newham sit at the top right, while Richmond upon Thames and Kingston upon Thames sit at the bottom left. The relationship is strongly linear, reinforcing that employment deprivation is not merely correlated with overall deprivation but is its primary driver.
An interesting outlier is Westminster, which sits mid-range on both axes despite having the highest total crime count in London. This is consistent with the Westminster Effect discussed in the exploratory analysis — high visitor footfall inflates crime counts independently of local deprivation levels.
4.5 Income Poverty and Health Outcomes
The income vs health scatter chart reveals a clear positive relationship between income deprivation and poor health outcomes across London boroughs. Hackney sits in the top right — high income deprivation and the worst health outcomes. Richmond upon Thames sits in the bottom left — low income deprivation and the best health outcomes. The colour coding confirms that overall deprivation tracks closely with both variables simultaneously.
One notable pattern is that several boroughs show relatively poor health outcomes despite moderate income deprivation — suggesting that health disadvantage in London is not purely a function of poverty and may reflect other structural factors such as housing quality, environmental conditions, or healthcare access. This points to a limitation of income-focused interventions and warrants further investigation.

4.6 Key Findings and Observations
Looking at the results as a whole, several patterns stood out. Some were expected, while others were less obvious from the individual datasets.
Council Tax and Unequal Living Conditions
One thing that stood out when looking at London boroughs together was how different living conditions can be despite residents living under the same London-wide system. People across all 32 boroughs pay council tax, but the data shows large differences between boroughs in areas such as health, employment, crime and the environment.
For example, Barking and Dagenham has considerably higher levels of deprivation than Richmond upon Thames. This does not mean that council tax directly causes or solves deprivation, but it does raise an interesting question about whether residents across London experience the same level of public services and quality of life despite contributing through the same local taxation system.
This is important because it shows that deprivation is not simply about an individual's circumstances. Where someone lives can make a significant difference to the conditions they experience.
Borough Averages Can Hide Inequality
Another important finding is that looking at deprivation at borough level does not show the full picture. Each borough is treated as one overall area in the analysis, but there can be large differences between neighbourhoods within the same borough.
Kensington and Chelsea is a good example. The borough contains some extremely wealthy areas but also areas with much higher levels of deprivation. Looking only at the borough's average can therefore make these differences less visible.
This is a limitation of the analysis, but it also shows why deprivation should not always be viewed simply as a problem affecting an entire borough. In some cases, the inequality exists within the borough itself.
Education Did Not Stand Out as Strongly as Expected
One finding that was less obvious was the position of education in the model. Education might be expected to have a strong relationship with deprivation because poorer areas can experience poorer educational outcomes.
However, education was not among the strongest features in the SHAP analysis. This suggests that, within this particular dataset and model, education was not as important for the model's deprivation predictions as some of the other variables.
This should not be interpreted as meaning that education is unimportant. Instead, it may indicate that the relationship between education and deprivation is more complicated than simply assuming that poorer boroughs will always have the poorest educational outcomes.
Crime: More Crime Does Not Automatically Mean More Deprivation
The results also showed that total recorded crime was not as strongly related to deprivation as might initially be expected.
Westminster, for example, has a very high number of recorded crimes, but this does not make it one of the most deprived boroughs. One possible explanation is that Westminster attracts very large numbers of workers, tourists and visitors, meaning that the number of people present in an area is much higher than its resident population.
This suggests that simply counting the number of recorded crimes may not give a complete picture of deprivation. The type of crime, where it occurs and how many people are present in an area can also affect the figures.
The crime deprivation score appeared more useful in the model than the raw crime count. This suggests that how crime is measured matters when examining its relationship with deprivation.
The East–West Pattern
The results also showed a clear geographical pattern across London.
Many of the more deprived boroughs were concentrated in east and inner-east London, while many of the less deprived boroughs were located in south-west and outer London.
This was one of the clearest patterns visible when comparing the boroughs. It suggests that deprivation in London is not randomly distributed across the city. Instead, there appears to be a geographical pattern, with some areas consistently experiencing higher levels of disadvantage.
However, this analysis only shows the pattern in the data used for this project. It does not by itself explain why the east-west difference exists.
Employment Was Particularly Important
One of the most important findings from the Random Forest and SHAP analysis was the importance of employment-related deprivation.
The model placed employment among the features that had a strong influence on its predictions of overall deprivation. This suggests that employment is closely connected with the wider deprivation patterns observed across the boroughs.
This finding is particularly useful because it gives a possible direction for further investigation. Instead of looking at deprivation only as a general measure of poverty, it suggests that access to employment and the labour market may be an important part of the problem.
However, the model cannot prove that employment deprivation causes overall deprivation. It only shows that employment-related variables were important when the model was making its predictions.
Overall Finding
Overall, the analysis shows that deprivation in London is not explained by one single factor. Income, employment, health, education, crime and environmental conditions are all connected to the wider picture, although their importance varies.
The geographical pattern was particularly clear, but the analysis also showed why looking at individual factors in isolation can be misleading. For example, high crime counts do not automatically mean high deprivation, while borough averages can hide major differences between neighbourhoods.
The most useful outcome of the analysis was therefore not simply identifying which borough was "most deprived." It was showing that London's deprivation is a combination of economic, social, health and environmental factors that vary considerably depending on where people live.
